Distance alone doesn't make a resource valuable --- risk does. A game that
puts its best minerals in a "dangerous" zone only creates a real economic
story if something can actually go wrong on the way there, and that
something usually means other players.

## Null-sec as a supply chain problem

EVE Online's null-security space produces the materials capital ships need,
and getting them to a market where they're worth something means running a
supply chain through space patrolled by player alliances who'd rather you
didn't. Every convoy is a risk calculation: bigger haul, bigger loss if it's
caught, and the price at the safe-space market has to be high enough to make
that risk worth taking in aggregate, across everyone who tries.

## Why this differs from a real-world supply chain

A real freight route's risk is roughly stable --- weather, piracy rates, and
insurance markets change slowly. A frontier game economy's risk is a
function of *who's currently paying attention*, which can flip in a day when
an alliance redeploys its fleet elsewhere. Prices in these markets are
consequently far more volatile than a comparable real commodity, and that
volatility is itself information: a sudden price spike in a frontier good is
often the first public signal that a supply route just got cut.

Trade Route Planning Workshop: given a simplified risk/reward map, plan a
